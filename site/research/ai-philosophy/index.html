The Philosophy Engine

Automating Philosophical Discovery Through Literature, Argument, and Counterargument

How an AI pipeline grounded in the philosophy literature found ideas for papers, developed them under AI review, and tested them against published work.

We built the pipeline for the AI Philosophy Competition. It starts from the journal literature: articles, grouped into debates by the works they cite. Inside a single debate, an AI maps the positions, tensions, open problems and unanswered objections, and proposes candidate theses that answer to that literature. This within-cluster route is the baseline, and all three submitted papers came from it. A variation, the cross-cluster route, combines debates that rarely cite each other in search of less conventional ideas. Surviving theses become memos and then papers under AI referees and an AI editor, and every accepted paper is compared with published papers from its debate and with hidden test papers held back from the author.

By Matthew Bonci, Lehan Zhang and David Zollikofer · Read the report as a PDF

The titles and texts of the essays, and the names of the debates they came from, are withheld until the competition’s results are announced.

Overview

How the system works, in five paragraphs

We began by grouping journal articles into literature clusters based on shared references: papers were connected when they cited sufficiently similar work. These clusters help identify ongoing conversations in the literature, though a cluster may contain several debates. We ranked them using proxies for which conversations might be ripe for a new contribution, including research activity, relevance, access to key literature and connections to other debates.

We explored two routes to finding ideas. In our within-cluster route, the baseline, AI uses a cluster’s catalogue and citation network to map positions, tensions, open problems and existing objections and replies. It reads selected literature and records source passages for its claims. In its fullest version it also reads five influential short papers in full, examining how they bound their claims, develop their arguments and engage the literature. Using the map and those examples, it proposes distinct candidate theses. Each states a thesis and an argument, explains its relationship to at least ten relevant papers of the cluster, and considers how the contribution might fit within the competition’s 6,000-word limit.

The cross-cluster route is a variation designed to elicit more unconventional, cross-domain ideas. AI mapped the literature of different clusters and combined elements, for example a method from a debate with an open problem from another. The combinations passed through a series of checks, and the strongest survivors were prepared as candidate theses. This route also produced papers the AI editor accepted, but none of them was among the three selected.

Next, the candidate theses enter the paper-development stage. Two AI referees assess each one. One checks the existing literature, including related fields, for work that may already contain the proposed claim; the other asks whether the argument is convincing and would matter to readers. When several survive, a ranker selects which advance. An AI author develops each selected thesis into a short working memo that spells out its argument, assumptions and consequences before the full paper is written. Four referees and, when enabled, a panel modelled on the competition’s judges assess each memo. The author responds to their criticism, and an editor decides which memos survive.

The pipeline then drafts a paper, checks and revises it, and compares it with published papers. If a paper is rejected, the pipeline can return to another surviving idea. Humans design the process and write the rules; AI proposes and develops the arguments, subject to checks and review. AI referees reviewed the candidate papers as if for a top journal, and on that basis the AI chose the three submitted.

1 · Literature

The pipeline starts from the literature: articles in debates, of which the best-ranked were packaged with their full texts

Philosophy proceeds in conversations, so the pipeline first finds them. Articles that cite overlapping work were linked, and a standard community-detection algorithm grouped them into clusters, which we call debates. A ranking script then scored each debate on research activity, relevance, access to key texts and connections to other debates, and the best-ranked were packaged with their full texts and citation graphs. The literature is not only the starting point: it is consulted, quoted and checked at every later stage.

1.1 · The question

Can an AI come up with a genuinely new idea in philosophy, and how would anyone check?

Language models can now write a passable philosophy paper. The hard part is the idea. Most ideas that sound new are not: someone has already published them, or they fall apart when examined closely. A pipeline that hopes to contribute must therefore know the literature it contributes to, and must try hard to kill its own ideas before it spends effort on them.

The project used two routes to new ideas. The within-cluster route, our baseline, stays inside one conversation: it compares the papers of a single debate to find overlooked points, open questions, unanswered objections and similar openings (section 2.1). All three submitted papers came from this route. The cross-cluster route is a variation of it, designed to elicit less conventional ideas: it takes two debates that rarely cite each other and combines something from one (a claim, a problem, a method) with something from the other (sections 2.2 to 2.6). Most such combinations are empty, because the two sides share a word but mean different things by it, or because a method is pointed at a problem it cannot handle; so this route proposes many combinations and then tries hard to kill each one. It produced candidate papers that the AI editor accepted, none of which was selected.

Both routes feed the same paper pipeline, which turns a candidate thesis into a memo and then a paper under AI review (section 3) and compares the accepted paper with published work (section 4).

Words you will meet

Debate (literature cluster): a group of journal articles whose reference lists overlap; a cluster may hold several debates. Candidate thesis: a short statement of a proposed claim, its argument and its closest existing work; the pipeline calls it a card. Debate map: an AI’s account of a debate’s positions, tensions, open problems and unanswered objections, tied to quotations. Item (cross-cluster route): one claim, problem or method from a debate, with a checked quotation. Combination (pairing): an item from a debate put together with an item from another. Gate: a test that can kill a combination. Seed: the starting number of a random draw, so anyone can repeat the draw exactly. Pivot: a thesis replaced outright while the idea is written up; it is screened again as a new candidate. Game: one blind comparison of two papers by an AI judge. Rubric: the judge’s instructions for the six criteria; it was revised once during the project.

1.2 · The design

A pipeline built to kill its own ideas, with every rule written down before it ran

Seven design choices, and why
  1. Ground every idea in the literature. Candidate theses are proposed from a map of a real debate, not from a topic name, so that each one answers to what has actually been argued.
  2. Check every quotation. A script looks for each quotation word for word in its source text and drops claims whose quotation is not found; the paper’s quotations are checked the same way.
  3. Search for prior art before spending effort. A prior-art referee screens every thesis, and the cross-cluster route adds a library search ranked by BM25, a public index and a web search. A partly anticipated claim may pass, with a note of what remains new.
  4. Rank comparatively. Screening scores bunch at 2 of 5, so when several theses survive, a ranker orders them side by side, three times in shuffled order.
  5. Treat a changed thesis as a new idea. A separate check compares each revised thesis with its predecessor; a replaced thesis goes back to screening.
  6. Keep test papers back. Hidden test papers are removed from the AI author’s library and blocked from retrieval, so the final test compares the paper with work it could not have drawn on.
  7. Fix the rules in advance. The rules live in code and prompts under version control; the cross-cluster generator was fingerprinted before its first draw.
1.3 · The debates

A script mapped debates from shared references and ranked them; the top were packaged by rule

A script linked articles from philosophy journals whose reference lists overlapped, and a standard clustering method grouped them into clusters; we call each cluster a debate. A script then ranked all of them on nine equally weighted measures in three groups: activity and relevance (recent output, share in top generalist journals, authorship by members of the judging committee, growth), researchability (access to core texts, reference coverage, stability of the cluster, access to short papers) and connections to other debates. The 15 highest-ranked debates, a cutoff set by hand, were packaged by rule. Each packet is a reading folder with the debate’s articles held in full text, its citation graph and its most-cited texts.

For the runs in which the paper pipeline mapped a debate itself, candidate debates went to four AI assessors, who judged the room for a contribution, the likely reception, the feasibility with our library and the risk. The standalone within-cluster generator started from the six top debates of the numerical ranking (section 2.1).

2.1 · The baseline: the within-cluster route

Inside a single debate, an AI maps what has been argued and proposes theses that answer to the map

Most contributions to philosophy answer a live question in an existing conversation: an overlooked distinction, an unanswered objection, an open problem. The within-cluster route builds that situation in. The AI reads a single debate, writes a map in four parts (positions, principles in tension, open problems, unanswered objections), and proposes candidate theses tied to entries of the map. Two implementations were used.

The paper pipeline’s own mapping session (papers A and C)

An AI author session reads the debate’s literature in the library, writes the debate map, and proposes candidate theses; a second card writer adds more. All of them enter screening (section 3.1). Paper A came from the second writer’s set, Paper C from the mapping session.

The standalone within-cluster generator (Paper K)

This generator adds explicit reading and grounding requirements. It started from the six top debates of the numerical ranking and kept those with at least five available papers of at most 6,000 words, so that the AI could study how short published papers bound their claims; three debates qualified. For each, it received a packet with the debate’s text-available papers, the membership catalogue, the citation graph and preselected readings. It had to read the five most-cited short papers in full, build the four-part map with every entry linked to source passages, and propose ten distinct candidate theses. Each thesis had to engage at least ten papers of its own debate, each through a quotation of at least 40 characters that a script then located in the stored text, and had to include a plan for a 6,000-word paper with a six-part word budget. A script checked the hashes, headings, map references, reading declarations, quotations and word budgets before the theses were handed on.

For Paper K’s debate, the generator worked from text-available papers, reported readings including in full, built a map of entries, and grounded its ten theses in verified passages. The ten theses entered the paper pipeline at screening; one became Paper K.

2.2 · The variation: combining two debates

The cross-cluster route first wrote down what each debate’s literature says, and a script checked every quotation

The cross-cluster route looks for ideas that a specialist in a single debate would not reach alone: a method from a debate applied to a problem in another, or an objection carried across. Each combination joins a home debate, which the new idea is about, and a donor debate, which lends it a method or an objection. To compare debates that use different vocabularies, the route first reduces each debate to items.

For each debate, one AI call read excerpts (the first 2,500 and last 1,200 words) of five core full texts and every article’s abstract, and listed items of seven kinds, such as positions, tensions and open problems. Each item has a plain statement, a field-neutral form (letters in place of the field’s terms, so that items from different fields can be lined up), two to five tags for its logical shape from a fixed list, and a supporting quotation. A script dropped of items because their quotation was not found word for word in the source.

How to read the item viewer

The first row of buttons picks a debate (the first two are the home and donor debates of the pair the run handed on, then the other 13 by code); the second row filters by kind of item, with the number of such items in the whole bank. 2.3 · Combinations

A random draw, fixed in advance, combined items across debates; most combinations were doomed

Three main draws produced combinations each. Each draw starts from a seed, a number fixed in advance, so anyone can repeat it; a fourth draw, later, stayed inside the top card’s pair of debates. Each combination follows one of three recipes, picked with weights of one half, one quarter and one quarter (realised: , and of ): one field’s method applied to another field’s open problem (O1); one field’s unanswered objection turned against a position in another (O2); two tensions with the same shape set side by side (O3). Gate K0 first dropped combinations whose two sources are already cited together by three or more papers.

2.4 · The gates

Seven gates in a row: a combination must pass each to reach the next

The gates are ordered from cheap to expensive. A script and a quick structural test remove the hopeless combinations first, so that the costlier searches and the cold critic are spent only on combinations with a chance.

From the bridge test’s prompt

Shown because the field-neutral test, stating both items with the same letters, is the route’s most distinctive idea, and because the prompt forbids killing a combination merely for being risky.

For each combination decide only whether it is hopeless. Do not kill a combination because its thesis would probably turn out false; high-risk combinations should pass. Kill (keep = false) only for one of these reasons: - equivocation: the items share a word but not a structure. Test: state both items with the SAME schematic letters; if either statement is then false of its field, it is equivocation; - preconditions_unmet (O1): the target lacks a structure the method requires […] - no_deliverable: nothing statable would come out (no theorem, countermodel, verdict, or changed argument); - trivial: the result is immediate or already standard practice in the home field; - incoherent: the items cannot be brought into contact at all.

The ranker (K3) ordered the survivors of each draw three times, shuffling the input order each time; the average place decides which six go on. 2.5 · The freeze

The cross-cluster generator’s rules were fingerprinted before the first real draw, and did not change

A rule that can be changed after the results are seen is not a rule. Before the first real draw, a script fingerprinted (hashed) the files that define the generator: every script, prompt and configuration, the item bank and the packets. Together they fix the model for each role, the seeds, the recipe weights, the kill rules and the top-card rule. After the draws, and again when this page was built, a script fingerprinted every file again; had changed. No human read a card from the real draws before the hand-off.

2.6 · The hand-off

One top card by a fixed rule, three companions from its pair of debates, and reading lists chosen by a script

Each paper is built from one pair of debates, so only one survivor goes on: the card with the best normalised rank across the main draws, as a fixed rule required. (Normalised rank is a card’s average place divided by the number of combinations ranked in its draw, so that draws of different sizes compare fairly.) The other survivors stop here. To give the paper stage a choice, more cards came from the same two debates: two from a fresh seeded draw of combinations inside that pair, and one top-up. Fixed rules chose the reading lists: five core papers, six comparison papers and three hidden test papers. From here the cards follow the same paper pipeline as every other candidate thesis.

3.1 · Screening

Two referees screen every candidate thesis; nine of Paper K’s ten were already argued in print

Screening is the cheapest place to stop a bad idea. Two AI referees read each candidate thesis with no other context: one checks for prior publication, searching the library and drawing on its own knowledge; the other reads sceptically. Each gives a verdict and a promise score from 1 to 5. A thesis dies if it is already published, has a fatal flaw, or is rejected. When several survive, a separate AI editor ranks them three times in shuffled order, because screening scores bunch: in three runs, each referee gave 2 of 5 in 148 of 174 reports. In Paper K’s run only one thesis survived, so no ranking was needed.

Table 6. Screening of the ten candidate theses in Paper K’s run. Each referee gives a verdict and a promise score from 1 to 5.

3.2 · The memo and its referees

Four referees raised objections to the memo; the author answered each, and the thesis changed enough to be screened again

Before a paper is written, a fresh AI author session writes a memo of two to three pages that spells out the argument, its assumptions and its consequences. Four AI referees, working separately (a field specialist, a prior-art referee, an argument critic and a sceptical reader), rate each objection fatal, major or minor and give a verdict. In some runs a fifth report came from a juror panel, AI readers modelled on the competition judges’ public articles; it was not used in the runs of the three submitted papers. The author answers every objection by its label, and an editor decides whether the memo survives.

Table 7. The four referee reports on Paper K’s memo, and the check of whether the thesis changed.

A changed thesis counts as a new idea. A separate check compares each memo’s thesis with its candidate thesis twice: when the memo is written, and again after the author’s reply to the referees. In Paper K’s run the thesis was the same when the memo was written but replaced after the reply, so the new thesis went back to the screening referees as a new candidate, and passed.

3.3 · The paper

Paper K’s first draft failed three scripted checks, passed after a fix, and was accepted after one round of review

The author writes the paper on a fixed template. Its brief asks for one central claim, a quarter of the words spent on framing, every term explained, and every formal result also stated in words. Scripts check that the paper compiles, stays under 5,700 words, holds most of the works it cites in full text, quotes them accurately, and contains no talk about its own writing process; a failed check sends the version back to the author. In the later runs, including Paper K’s, an outside reader given nothing but the paper reports where it loses the thread. Up to two rounds of referee reports and revisions follow, and an editor who never sees the paper’s history accepts, asks for revision or rejects.

4.1 · The games

An AI judge from another developer compared Paper K with nine published papers, each both ways round

Each comparison is called a game. The judge reads two papers without author names and says which is better on each of the competition’s six criteria, then reads them again in the opposite order, so that the order of reading cannot decide the result. During revision, games against three of the published comparison papers decide whether a revision is kept, and the author is shown the judge’s account of what those papers do better. The accepted version then plays all six comparison papers from its debate, including those three, and finally the three hidden test papers that were kept out of the author’s library. Only the hidden papers played no part in development, so they are the cleaner test.

The rubric changed once during the project. Under the first rubric the top level of each criterion read “a top journal would accept”; the revised rubric’s levels name no venue. Papers A and C were judged under the first rubric and Paper K under the revised one, by the same judge model. Results under different rubrics are therefore reported side by side but not compared.

Table 10. Paper K’s points per criterion: against the six comparison papers (12 games) and against the three hidden test papers (6 games). A win scores one point and a tie half.

What the judge gives Noûs papers

The rubric’s levels are relative, not a verdict of any journal. In earlier runs, under the first rubric, the gpt-6.1-sol judge scored nine published papers 382 times. Papers from Noûs and The Philosophical Review never received the top level on argument or scholarship. On average the generated essays of those runs scored above the published papers on clarity, argument and scholarship, and below the Noûs and Philosophical Review papers on significance, originality and literature.

Version control and records

The paper pipeline is kept in a Git repository, and its runs record the code version they used. The cross-cluster generator’s code, prompts and configuration were fingerprinted before its first draw, and the within-cluster generator’s packages carry file fingerprints. Every model call is logged with its role and cost. Together these let each submitted paper be traced to the code, prompts and records that produced it.

Subscription calls are valued at list prices, so the costs are estimates, not invoices; they cover all three team members’ work and all trials, including runs that produced no submitted paper.

6.3 · The answer

A machine can find, prune and develop ideas grounded in the literature, on the record; whether the results are new and good is for philosophers to judge

Section 1.1 asked whether an AI can come up with a genuinely new idea in philosophy, and how anyone would check. Here is what the runs show, and what would settle the rest.

What the runs show

  • A process written down in advance can map a debate, propose theses that answer to its literature, and kill most of them with stated reasons: of the theses in Paper K’s run died at screening as already argued in print.
  • Every idea, objection, reply and sentence was written by AI models; humans built the process, wrote its rules, restarted runs, and wrote the instruction for the final review, on the basis of which the AI chose the three submitted papers.
  • Each of the three submitted papers was accepted by the AI editor and won every game it played against hidden test papers.
  • The process leaves a complete, checkable record.

What would settle it

  • The judgement of philosophers, starting with the competition’s own judging.

Disclosures

  • The author, referees and editor of the three submitted papers are Claude Opus 5.5; their game judge is gpt-6.1-sol, from a different developer.
  • Where it was used, the juror panel modelled the competition’s judges from their public articles, named them only by label, and added objections, never scores. It was not used in the runs of the three submitted papers.
  • The rubric is derived from the competition’s six criteria. Its levels are relative: under the first rubric the judge never gave its top level to a published paper on argument or scholarship, in 382 scores that include five papers from Noûs and The Philosophical Review.
  • In the cross-cluster route the starting debate map was built by a script from the item bank, not by an AI author reading the debate.
  • Quotations in the item bank come from licensed texts and are short (at most 43 words; the rule asks for 8 to 40).
